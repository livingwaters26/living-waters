Genesis

Everything the rest of Scripture assumes is established here: who God is, what humanity is for, what went wrong, and why a promise to one family matters.

Traditional author
Moses, with later editorial shaping widely recognised
Composition
Material ancient; final form usually dated to the monarchy or exile
First audience
Israel — a people needing to know whose they are
Setting
From creation to Joseph's coffin in Egypt
Genre
Theological narrative, genealogy, covenant record
Structure key
Ten toledot — 'these are the generations of'

Context

Genesis was written for people who already knew other creation stories. The Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Atrahasis epic told of gods at war, of humanity made as slave labour to relieve divine boredom, of a flood sent because people were noisy. Genesis answers those stories point for point — and the differences are the argument. There is no combat. There is no rival. Humanity is not a workforce but an image. The flood comes because of violence, not noise.

The book is built on a repeated formula: elleh toledot, 'these are the generations of.' It appears ten times and it is the spine of the whole thing. Genesis is not organised by chronology or by theme but by descent — the story keeps narrowing, from cosmos to earth, earth to humanity, humanity to Noah, Noah to Abraham, Abraham to Judah. By the last chapter the camera has moved from the whole universe to one coffin in Egypt. That narrowing is deliberate: it is how a promise to bless all nations gets carried.

Key themes

Creation as gift, not conquest

God makes by speaking. No struggle, no defeated enemy, no material to fight against. The sea monsters of 1:21 — the very creatures other cultures made into rival gods — are listed as things God simply made and called good.

The image of God

In the ancient Near East, only the king bore the divine image. Genesis 1:27 gives it to everyone, male and female. That single move underwrites every later claim about human dignity in the Bible.

Blessing under threat

God blesses; something moves to unmake the blessing. Fruitfulness meets murder, the flood, Babel, barrenness. The pattern sets up the whole canon: promise made, promise endangered, promise kept anyway.

Covenant with one, for all

Genesis 12:3 is the hinge. Abraham is chosen not instead of the nations but for them. Election here is instrumental, not preferential — a point Paul spends most of Romans 4 and 9 making.

God works through the wrong people

Every patriarch lies. Jacob cheats his way to the blessing. Judah is a disaster. The book never sanitises them, and it never suggests the promise depended on them being better.

Providence without spectacle

The Joseph cycle contains almost no miracles. God's action is visible only in retrospect — 'ye meant evil, but God meant it for good' (50:20) — which is how most people actually experience providence.

How the book moves

1–11, the world. Creation, fall, flood, Babel. Four crises, each answered by grace rather than by abandonment. The section closes with humanity scattered and a genealogy running to Abram.

12–25, Abraham. A call, a promise, and twenty-five years of waiting for one child. The covenant is cut in chapter 15, sealed in 17, and tested in 22.

25–36, Isaac and Jacob. The promise passes through the younger, the weaker, the deceiver. Jacob wrestles at the Jabbok and comes away renamed and limping.

37–50, Joseph. The longest sustained narrative in the book, and the one with the least direct divine speech. The family that will become Israel ends up in Egypt — which is exactly where Exodus needs them.

Verse-by-verse insights

Genesis 1:1

In the beginning God created

The verb bara is used in Hebrew only with God as its subject. No human ever bara-s anything. The word itself makes a claim: this kind of making belongs to God alone. And note what is missing — no account of God's own origin. Scripture opens by refusing the question.

bara' (baw-RAH) — to create; grammatically reserved for divine action

Genesis 1:2

Waste and void

Tohu wa-bohu — a rhyming pair meaning something like 'formless and empty.' Chapters 1:3–13 fix the formlessness by separating; 1:14–31 fix the emptiness by filling. Days 1–3 build the containers, days 4–6 populate them. The structure is deliberate, and reading it as a pattern rather than a clock resolves more problems than it creates.

tohu wa-bohu (TOH-hoo vah-VOH-hoo) — unformed and unfilled

Genesis 1:26–27

Let us make man in our image

The plural has been read three ways: a heavenly court, a plural of deliberation, or a hint of the Trinity. The Fathers took the third; most Hebraists take the first two. What is not in dispute is the effect of verse 27 — image-bearing extends to the whole species, and to both sexes, without qualification.

tselem (TSEH-lem) — image, representation — used elsewhere of royal statues

Genesis 2:7

Breathed into his nostrils

The man is dust plus breath. Not a soul housed in a body but a body made alive. This matters later: the biblical hope is resurrection, not escape from the physical — a point Paul presses in 1 Corinthians 15 and John assumes throughout.

nephesh chayyah (NEH-fesh khah-YAH) — living being; the same phrase is used of animals in 1:20

Genesis 3:1

Now the serpent was more subtle

The text calls it a creature God made. It offers no origin story, no fallen-angel backstory, no cosmic dualism. The identification with Satan comes later — Revelation 12:9 and Romans 16:20 make it — but Genesis itself declines to explain evil. It only shows what evil says.

'arum (ah-ROOM) — shrewd, crafty — a wordplay with 'arummim, 'naked,' in 2:25

Genesis 3:15

He shall bruise thy head

The protoevangelium, the first gospel. Christian reading from Irenaeus onward takes the seed of the woman as Christ. The Hebrew is a collective noun and can equally mean humanity in general — but the singular pronoun that follows has carried the messianic reading for two thousand years.

zera' (ZEH-rah) — seed, offspring — singular in form, often collective in sense

Genesis 6:5–8

But Noah found grace

The flood narrative is bracketed by two statements about God's heart, not humanity's. Verse 6 says God grieved. This is the first appearance of a theme the prophets will build careers on: judgment that costs the judge something.

nacham (naw-KHAM) — to be sorry, to relent — the same verb used of God's compassion

Genesis 12:1–3

In thee shall all the families of the earth be blessed

Seven clauses, five of them blessing. After eleven chapters of scattering, the answer to Babel is one man told to leave. Paul calls this verse the gospel preached beforehand (Galatians 3:8) — which tells you how early the church saw the whole arc.

barak (baw-RAK) — to bless; the root appears five times in three verses

Genesis 15:6

He believed; and it was counted unto him for righteousness

The single most quoted Old Testament verse in Paul's argument for justification by faith (Romans 4:3, Galatians 3:6) and the verse James uses to argue the opposite-sounding case (James 2:23). Both are reading the same Hebrew. The tension between them shaped the Reformation.

chashab (khaw-SHAB) — to reckon, to credit — a bookkeeping term

Genesis 22:8

God will provide himself a lamb

The Hebrew can be read 'God will provide for himself the lamb' — and the ram that appears in verse 13 is a ram, not a lamb. Early Christian readers noticed the gap and filled it with John 1:29. Jewish tradition, the Akedah, reads the same passage as the paradigm of faithful obedience.

yireh (yir-EH) — he will see / he will provide — the root behind Jehovah-jireh

Genesis 32:28

Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel

The nation is named after a night of wrestling that leaves its founder limping. Whatever else Israel means, the text insists it means one who struggles with God and survives — not one who submits easily.

Yisra'el (yis-raw-ALE) — he strives with God

Genesis 50:20

Ye meant evil against me; but God meant it for good

The theological summary of the whole book, spoken by a man with every reason to be bitter. Note the sentence does not say the evil was good, or that it was not evil. Two intentions run through one set of events. Genesis ends without resolving how.

chashab (khaw-SHAB) — the same verb as 15:6 — 'to reckon, to intend'

Historical development

Early view

The Fathers read Genesis christologically as a matter of course. Irenaeus takes 3:15 as the first announcement of Christ; Justin Martyr reads the three visitors of chapter 18 as a theophany. On the days of creation they split early — Basil (c. 370) argues for literal days in his Hexaemeron, while Origen and later Augustine treat the sequence as a literary and theological ordering rather than a timetable.

When it changed

Two shifts, seventeen centuries apart. Augustine's De Genesi ad litteram (c. 415) made non-literal readings respectable in the West and warned that badly argued science does the church real damage. Then in the nineteenth century geology and Darwin forced the question in a new form, and what had been an in-house exegetical debate became an identity marker.

Who changed it

The modern young-earth position as a movement traces largely to Seventh-day Adventist writer George McCready Price and, decisively, to Whitcomb and Morris's The Genesis Flood (1961), which fused flood geology with biblical inerrancy. Before 1961 most conservative Protestants — including the authors of The Fundamentals — held old-earth or day-age views without controversy. The current alignment is younger than most people assume.
