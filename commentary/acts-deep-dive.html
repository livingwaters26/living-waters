Acts

How a frightened group of Galileans in a locked room became a movement that reached the capital of the empire — and what it cost them to admit that Gentiles belonged.

Author
Luke, companion of Paul; volume two of a two-part work
Date
Usually 80–90; some argue early 60s from the abrupt ending
Addressed to
Theophilus — patron, catechumen, or both
Span covered
c. AD 30 to c. AD 62, roughly thirty years
Genre
Hellenistic historiography with speeches as interpretation
Structure key
1:8 — Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, the ends of the earth

Context

Acts is the second half of a single work. Luke's Gospel ends with the disciples in the temple; Acts begins in the same place and then moves relentlessly outward. The programme is stated in 1:8 and the book follows it exactly: chapters 1–7 in Jerusalem, 8–12 into Judea and Samaria, 13–28 to the ends of the earth, ending in Rome.

Nearly a third of the book is speeches. That is not padding — it is how ancient historians did interpretation. Peter at Pentecost, Stephen before the council, Paul at Athens and before Agrippa: these speeches tell you what Luke thinks the events mean. Read the narrative for what happened and the speeches for why it mattered.

Key themes

The Spirit drives the plot

Every major expansion is initiated by the Spirit, not by strategy. The church does not plan its way to the Gentiles; it is pushed, and usually it argues first.

Reluctant inclusion

The most important thing Acts records is an argument. Peter needs a vision three times (ch. 10) and still has to defend himself (ch. 11). The council of chapter 15 exists because the question would not go away.

Persecution as accelerant

Every attempt to stop the movement spreads it. Stephen's death scatters the church into Samaria; Paul's imprisonment gets him a free passage to Rome and a captive audience.

Continuity with Israel

The speeches are saturated with Scripture. Luke's claim is not that a new religion started but that Israel's story reached its point — and that the resistance came from inside, as it always had.

Rome is not the enemy

Luke repeatedly shows Roman officials finding no crime. Whether that is apologetic, evidence of a real legal record, or both, it shapes how the whole book reads.

The unfinished ending

Paul preaches in Rome 'no man forbidding him' — and Luke stops. No verdict, no execution, no summary. The most likely explanation is the most interesting one: the story is not over.

How the book moves

1–7, Jerusalem. Ascension, Pentecost, the first community, the first conflicts, and the first martyr. Stephen's speech is the longest in the book and the most confrontational.

8–12, outward. Philip and the Ethiopian, Saul's conversion, Peter and Cornelius. The theological centre of gravity shifts.

13–15, the crisis. Paul's first journey, then the Jerusalem council — the moment the church decides Gentiles do not have to become Jews first.

16–20, into Europe. Philippi, Thessalonica, Athens, Corinth, Ephesus. Luke's 'we' passages begin, suggesting eyewitness presence.

21–28, to Rome in chains. Arrest, five defences, shipwreck, and a rented house in Rome where the book simply stops.

Verse-by-verse insights

Acts 1:8

Ye shall be my witnesses

The book's table of contents, spoken by Jesus before he leaves. Note it answers a different question than the one asked in verse 6 — they ask about restoring the kingdom to Israel, and he redirects to witness. Luke does not record him denying the premise.

martys (MAR-tooss) — witness — the word that later becomes 'martyr,' for obvious reasons

Acts 2:4

They began to speak with other tongues

Luke is explicit in verses 6–11 that these are known human languages, understood by the hearers. Whatever later phenomena are called by the same name, Pentecost as Luke describes it is a reversal of Babel: not confusion scattered but comprehension gathered.

glossa (GLOSS-ah) — tongue, language

Acts 2:36

God hath made him both Lord and Christ

The climax of the first Christian sermon, and it lands as an accusation: 'this Jesus whom ye crucified.' Peter, who denied him seven weeks earlier, is the one saying it. Luke does not remark on the irony but he has clearly arranged it.

kyrios (KOO-ree-oss) — Lord — the Septuagint's word for the divine name, applied here to Jesus

Acts 2:38

Repent ye, and be baptized… unto the remission of your sins

One preposition, eis, carries centuries of division. It can mean 'in order to obtain' or 'with reference to.' Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran and Churches of Christ readings lean instrumental; most Baptist and Reformed readings lean declarative. Greek grammar alone does not settle it, and honest commentators on both sides admit so.

eis (ice) — into, unto, for — directional, and genuinely ambiguous here

Acts 4:12

None other name under heaven

Spoken to the Sanhedrin by unschooled men who had just healed a man they could not have healed. Luke notes in verse 13 that the council recognised them as having been with Jesus — the boldness is the evidence, not the argument.

sozo (SODE-zoh) — to save, to heal — the same verb covers both in verses 9 and 12

Acts 7:56

I see the heavens opened, and the Son of man standing

Stephen's vision. Every other New Testament reference has Christ seated at the right hand. Here he is standing. Readers have taken it as the judge rising to receive a witness, or the advocate standing to plead. Either way the detail is deliberate.

histemi (HIS-tay-mee) — to stand — the perfect participle, standing and remaining so

Acts 9:4

Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?

Not 'my people' but 'me.' The identification of Christ with his church, which Paul will spend a career developing into the theology of the body, begins as a sentence spoken to him on a road while he is lying in the dust.

Acts 10:15

What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common

The vision is about food. The application, as Peter works out in verse 28, is about people. Luke tells this story twice — once as narrative, once as Peter's testimony in chapter 11 — because he knows how hard the church found it.

koinos (koy-NOSS) — common, unclean — literally 'shared,' which is the irony

Acts 15:19

My sentence is, that we trouble not them

James's ruling at the Jerusalem council. The decision is not that the law was wrong but that Gentiles were never under it. The four restrictions that follow are pastoral, aimed at table fellowship, not a new minimum law.

parenochleo (par-en-okh-LEH-oh) — to trouble, to add burden to

Acts 17:23

Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you

Paul at the Areopagus quotes two pagan poets and cites no Scripture. The method is entirely different from his synagogue preaching and the church has argued about it since — is this contextual brilliance or a compromise Paul later regretted (1 Corinthians 2:1–2)? Luke offers no verdict.

agnostos (ag-NOCE-toss) — unknown — the root of 'agnostic'

Acts 20:35

It is more blessed to give than to receive

A saying of Jesus found in no Gospel. Its presence here is a quiet reminder that the written Gospels preserve a selection, not the whole — a point John states outright at 21:25.

Acts 28:31

No man forbidding him

The last two words in Greek, akolytos — 'unhindered.' Luke ends a book full of arrests, beatings, and shipwrecks on the word 'unhindered,' with the gospel being preached in the empire's capital. The lack of an ending is the ending.

akolytos (ak-o-LOO-toce) — without hindrance — the final word of the book

Historical development

Early view

The Fathers read Acts 2:38 straightforwardly: baptism is where forgiveness is received and the Spirit given. Cyril of Jerusalem, Tertullian, and the baptismal liturgies all assume it. Pentecost was celebrated as the church's birthday from very early on.

When it changed

Zwingli, in the 1520s, broke from both Rome and Luther by treating baptism as a sign of covenant membership rather than a means of grace. That move — not Luther's — created the reading of Acts 2:38 as declarative, and it spread through the Reformed and later Baptist traditions.

Who changed it

Zwingli first, then the Anabaptists from a different direction, insisting on believer's baptism while agreeing it was symbolic. The nineteenth-century Restoration Movement under Alexander Campbell then pushed hard the other way, making baptism-for-remission a defining doctrine. Three positions, one preposition, and no resolution in sight.
