Romans

Paul's most sustained argument, written to a church he had never visited, about how God can forgive the guilty without ceasing to be just.

Author
Paul, dictated to Tertius (16:22)
Date
c. AD 57, from Corinth, before the trip to Jerusalem
Audience
A mixed Jewish and Gentile church in Rome, not founded by Paul
Immediate occasion
Support for a planned mission to Spain (15:24)
Underlying occasion
Jewish believers returning after Claudius's expulsion, to a church that had become largely Gentile
Genre
Letter, but structured as sustained argument

Context

Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome around AD 49. When the edict lapsed and Jewish believers came back, they returned to congregations that had carried on without them and had become thoroughly Gentile. Chapters 14 and 15 — with their arguments about food, holy days, and who is judging whom — are not abstract ethics. They are Paul addressing a specific and awkward reunion.

That context reshapes the whole letter. The famous doctrinal chapters are not a systematic theology written in a vacuum; they are the foundation Paul lays for a practical problem. If Jew and Gentile are justified the same way, on the same terms, by the same faith, then neither group can look down on the other. Chapters 1–11 exist to make chapters 12–15 possible.

Key themes

The righteousness of God

The letter's key phrase, used about eight times. Luther's discovery was that it names a righteousness God gives, not only the standard he demands — though the phrase probably carries God's own covenant faithfulness as well.

No distinction

Repeated at 3:22 and 10:12. Everyone sinned; everyone is justified the same way. The phrase does double duty as doctrine and as social correction.

Union with Christ

Chapter 6 answers the obvious objection to grace — why not sin more? — not with a rule but with a fact: you died. 'In Christ' appears throughout and is arguably Paul's central category.

The problem of Israel

Chapters 9–11 are not a digression. If God's promises to Israel can fail, no promise is safe. Paul's answer runs through election, remnant, hardening, and ends in doxology rather than in a solved equation.

Life in the Spirit

Chapter 8 turns from the impasse of chapter 7 to the Spirit, and from personal deliverance to the groaning of creation itself. It is the widest angle in the letter.

Worship as ethics

12:1 makes the body the sacrifice and daily life the liturgy. Everything practical in the letter hangs from that one image.

How the book moves

1:1–17, thesis. The gospel is God's power for salvation, to Jew first and also to Greek.

1:18–3:20, the indictment. Gentiles condemned, then the moralist, then Israel. Nobody is left standing.

3:21–5:21, justification. The hinge. Righteousness apart from law, demonstrated from Abraham, secured in Christ.

6–8, the new life. Dead to sin, dead to the law, alive by the Spirit. Chapter 7's 'wretched man' and chapter 8's 'no condemnation' sit back to back on purpose.

9–11, Israel. Election, remnant, and grafting. Paul ends with a warning to Gentile readers against arrogance.

12–16, the body. Gifts, government, the weak and the strong, and a long list of names — a third of them women.

Verse-by-verse insights

Romans 1:16–17

The just shall live by faith

Quoting Habakkuk 2:4. Luther's account of reading this verse — 'I felt myself to be reborn and to have gone through open doors into paradise' — is the origin story of the Reformation. What changed for him was the genitive: not the righteousness God requires, but the righteousness God supplies.

dikaiosyne theou (dik-ai-oh-SOO-nay theh-OO) — righteousness of God — subjective or objective genitive, and the whole debate hangs on which

Romans 3:20

By the law is the knowledge of sin

The conclusion of the indictment. Paul's point is not that law is bad but that it was never the mechanism of rescue. It is diagnostic. The New Perspective adds that 'works of the law' may specifically mean the boundary markers — circumcision, food, sabbath — that separated Jew from Gentile.

erga nomou (ER-gah NOM-oo) — works of law — whether this means law-keeping generally or covenant badges specifically is the live question

Romans 3:24

Justified freely by his grace

Dorean, the same word Jesus uses in John 15:25 for being hated 'without a cause.' Gratuitously, for no reason arising from the recipient. Paul stacks three metaphors in two verses: the courtroom (justified), the slave market (redemption), and the temple (propitiation).

dorean (do-reh-AHN) — as a gift, for nothing, without cause

Romans 3:25

Whom God set forth to be a propitiation

Hilasterion is the Septuagint's word for the mercy seat, the lid of the ark where blood was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement. Paul is not merely saying the cross appeases God; he is saying the cross is the mercy seat, now set out in public where the old one was hidden behind a veil.

hilasterion (hil-as-TAY-ree-on) — mercy seat, place of atonement — Leviticus 16:14 in Greek

Romans 3:26

That he might himself be just, and the justifier

The sentence that states the problem the whole letter exists to solve. A judge who acquits the guilty is corrupt. Paul's claim is that the cross lets God do it without corruption — and 'the passing over of the sins done aforetime' concedes that until then there was an unresolved account.

paresis (PAR-es-is) — a passing over, a temporary suspension — not the same word as aphesis, forgiveness

Romans 5:12

Death passed unto all men, for that all sinned

The Greek eph' ho is notoriously ambiguous. The Latin Vulgate rendered it 'in whom,' which Augustine read as all sinning in Adam, and that reading built Western original sin. The Greek East read 'because,' yielding inherited mortality rather than inherited guilt. One preposition, two anthropologies.

eph' ho (eff HO) — because / in whom — the fault line between East and West

Romans 6:3–4

Buried with him by baptism into death

Paul's answer to antinomianism is not a warning but an identity. You cannot go on in what you died to. Note that baptism here is descriptive of a union already real, which is why this passage gets cited by every side of the baptism debate.

synthapto (soon-THAP-toh) — to bury together with

Romans 7:24

O wretched man that I am!

Is Paul describing himself as a Christian, as a pre-Christian Jew, or Israel under the law? Augustine changed his mind mid-career and landed on the Christian reading; the Greek Fathers generally did not. Your answer determines your whole doctrine of sanctification, which is why the argument never dies.

talaiporos (tal-AH-ee-po-ross) — wretched, miserable, worn out by struggle

Romans 8:1

There is therefore now no condemnation

The 'therefore' reaches back over the whole argument, not just chapter 7. And 'now' is temporal — the verdict of the last day has been announced early. Everything in chapter 8 flows from a sentence already passed.

katakrima (kat-AK-ree-mah) — condemnation, the sentence following a verdict

Romans 8:28

All things work together for good

Frequently misquoted as a promise that everything will turn out pleasantly. Verse 29 defines 'good' immediately: conformity to the image of Christ. The promise is real and it is not about comfort.

synergeo (soon-erg-EH-oh) — to work together with

Romans 9:16

Not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth

The verse Calvin and Arminius both built on. Note where Paul goes next: not to a theory of individual salvation but to Israel's history, and then in chapter 11 to a warning against Gentile presumption. The pastoral aim shapes the doctrine.

eleeo (el-eh-EH-oh) — to show mercy

Romans 12:1

Present your bodies a living sacrifice

The turn from argument to life. Logikos — reasonable, rational, or spiritual — describes worship that follows logically from chapters 1–11. Bodies, not souls: for Paul, worship is what you do with your physical life.

logike latreia (log-ik-AY lat-RY-ah) — reasonable service / spiritual worship

Historical development

Early view

The Greek Fathers read Romans through the lens of participation and healing more than of courtroom acquittal. Chrysostom's homilies stress grace and gift but do not frame justification forensically. The Latin West, following Augustine and the Vulgate's rendering of 5:12, developed the doctrines of original sin and inherited guilt that the East never adopted.

When it changed

Luther's lectures on Romans in 1515–16 and the breakthrough on 1:17 made justification a strictly declarative act — an alien righteousness imputed, not infused. Trent's sixth session (1547) responded by anathematising imputation-only and affirming justification as real inward renewal. The confessional line held for four centuries.

Who changed it

Two later disruptions. The 1999 Joint Declaration between Lutherans and Catholics found substantial agreement on the basics while leaving real differences intact. Separately, from 1977 onward, E. P. Sanders, James Dunn, and N. T. Wright's New Perspective argued that first-century Judaism was not a works-righteousness religion and that 'works of the law' meant covenant boundary markers. That reading is contested — but it reopened a question everyone had assumed was closed.
