Genesis 22

The Binding of Isaac

God tests Abraham with the most severe command in the Bible: sacrifice the promised son. Abraham obeys to the very last moment before God stops his hand and provides a ram instead — a single tight narrative that becomes one of Scripture's clearest pictures of substitutionary provision.
🔗 RelatedIsaac carrying the wood for his own sacrifice up the mountain (v.6) is widely read across traditions as a striking foreshadowing of Christ carrying His own cross to Golgotha — a connection explored further in Messianic Prophecy.

The Command and the Provision

Genesis 22:1-14
2...Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest... and offer him there for a burnt offering... 7...My father... Behold the fire and the wood: but where is the lamb for a burnt offering? 8And Abraham said, My son, God will provide himself a lamb... 12...now I know that thou fearest God, seeing thou hast not withheld thy son... from me. 13...behold behind him a ram caught in a thicket by his horns... 14And Abraham called the name of that place Jehovahjireh.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six affirm this was a real test God gave to reveal (both to Abraham and to the reader) the genuineness of his faith — not because God needed the information, since He already knew — and that God's actual intention from the start was provision, not the death of the promised son.
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: The historic conversation about this text concerns how it relates to Genesis 15:6's justification by faith — Catholic and Protestant traditions alike affirm Abraham was already justified by faith years earlier in ch.15, and read ch.22 as the visible proof and fruit of that already-existing faith (the reading James 2:21-23 gives explicitly), rather than a second, separate basis of righteousness.
Catholic
The Catechism (CCC 2572) reads Abraham's obedience here as brought "to perfection" by this test, and the whole episode as foreshadowing "the offering of the only Son of the Father" — a real historical event carrying forward typological weight toward Calvary without diminishing its own reality as something Abraham actually lived through.

Non-Denom.
Armstrong's material draws the parallel directly: a father, a beloved and only son of promise, a three-day journey, wood carried by the son himself up the mountain, and a substitute provided at the last moment — details too specific and numerous, in his reading, to be coincidental foreshadowing of the cross nearly two thousand years later.
