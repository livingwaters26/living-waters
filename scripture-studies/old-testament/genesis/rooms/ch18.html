Genesis 18-19

Sodom and Gomorrah

Abraham bargains with God over the fate of two cities, whittling the required number of righteous people down from fifty to ten. None are found. Lot and his family are pulled out just before judgment falls — a sober narrative about intercession, divine patience, and the real cost of a life built too close to compromise.
🔗 RelatedSodom becomes a fixed reference point for divine judgment throughout the rest of Scripture — Jesus Himself invokes it (Matthew 10:15, Luke 17:28-32), and Jude 7 names it explicitly as a warning example.

Abraham's Intercession

Genesis 18:16-33
23...Wilt thou also destroy the righteous with the wicked? 25...Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right? 32...I will not destroy it for ten's sake.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six affirm this as a genuine, God-honored act of intercessory prayer — Abraham doesn't presume to overrule God, he appeals to God's own character ("Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?"), and God takes the appeal seriously rather than rebuking it.
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: Traditions differ somewhat on what the descending-number bargain (fifty down to ten) is chiefly meant to teach — persistence in prayer, the value God places on even a small righteous remnant, or God's patience being far greater than human patience — without treating these readings as mutually exclusive.
Catholic
Catholic spiritual tradition has long held up this passage as a model of intercessory prayer's boldness and its limits — Abraham prays with real confidence yet also real humility ("I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes," v.27), a balance the Church commends in all prayer of intercession.

Judgment and Lot's Rescue

Genesis 19:1-29
1And there came two angels to Sodom at even... 15...Arise, take thy wife, and thy two daughters... lest thou be consumed in the iniquity of the city. 16And while he lingered, the men laid hold upon his hand... the LORD being merciful unto him... 24Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six affirm real, historical judgment fell on cities whose wickedness — including the coercive mob assault described in this chapter, and, as named later in Ezekiel 16:49-50, pride, excess, and a hardened neglect of the poor and needy — had become severe, while affirming God's mercy is what actually got Lot's family out (v.16, "while he lingered... the LORD being merciful").
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: Some traditions foreground the assault narrated directly in this chapter as the presenting sin in view; others foreground the fuller pattern Ezekiel later names for the whole city's guilt; both readings appear across all six traditions as complementary rather than competing, since Scripture itself supplies both descriptions of Sodom's sin.
Catholic
Catholic teaching reads this narrative as historical judgment on grave sin while consistently emphasizing v.16's detail that Lot was saved by God's initiative and mercy despite his own hesitation ("while he lingered") — salvation here, as elsewhere in Genesis, comes by grace reaching into human reluctance, not by human merit.

Lot's Wife and the Cost of Looking Back

Genesis 19:15-26
17...Escape for thy life; look not behind thee... 26But his wife looked back from behind him, and she became a pillar of salt.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six read this detail as a sober warning about incomplete, backward-looking obedience — Jesus reuses it directly as a warning to His own disciples (Luke 17:32) about the danger of clinging to a life that judgment has already claimed.
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: There is no significant doctrinal divergence among the six traditions on this specific verse; it is applied with substantially the same force across all of them.
Catholic
Catholic commentary reads Lot's wife's fate alongside her family's broader reluctance (v.16, needing to be physically taken by the hand) as illustrating how attachment to a sinful, doomed way of life can undo even a rescue that has already begun — a caution about half-hearted repentance.
