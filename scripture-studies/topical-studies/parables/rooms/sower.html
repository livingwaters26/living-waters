The Sower

Matthew 13:1–23 · Mark 4:1–20 · Luke 8:4–15

🟡 Moderate Divergence
Context. Taught early in Jesus's Galilean ministry, from a boat, to a crowd pressed along the shore. A farmer scatters seed by hand across a field with a footpath cut through it, patches of shallow limestone soil, and thorn roots still in the ground — an everyday scene made to carry the whole question of how people receive God's word.
One seed, one sower, four soils — the outcome depends entirely on the ground it lands in.

🛤️ The Path

Trampled hard by foot traffic; the seed never gets in at all. The word is heard but not understood, and the enemy snatches it away almost immediately (Matt 13:19).

🪨 Rocky Ground

A thin layer of soil over limestone bedrock — quick, eager sprouting, but no root depth. Joy at first, then collapse the moment trouble or persecution arrives (Matt 13:20–21).

🌵 Thorns

Soil that also grows weeds. The word takes root but gets choked out by "the cares of the world and the deceitfulness of riches" (Matt 13:22) — not opposition, but competition.

🌾 Good Soil

Hears, understands, and bears fruit — thirty, sixty, a hundredfold. Jesus doesn't flatten the yield; different good soil still produces at different levels.

🟡 Why traditions diverge here

The core reading — this parable is about how receptive different hearts are to God's word — is close to universal across all six traditions. The divergence shows up specifically in the rocky-ground soil: someone who "believes for a while, then falls away in time of testing" (Luke 8:13).

Reformed/Baptist readings typically treat rocky-ground faith as never having been genuine saving faith at all — a real response, but not regeneration, consistent with the perseverance of the saints. Catholic, Orthodox, and Wesleyan/Arminian-leaning Evangelical readings are more willing to treat it as real but temporary faith that is subsequently lost — consistent with a synergistic view of grace that can be resisted or abandoned after a genuine start. Both sides agree on what the text says; they disagree on what it implies about the nature of the faith described.
