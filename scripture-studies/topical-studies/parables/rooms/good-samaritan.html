🩹

The Good Samaritan

Luke 10:25–37

🟡 Moderate Divergence
Context. A lawyer, testing Jesus, asks what's required to inherit eternal life; Jesus turns the question back on the Law (love God, love neighbor), and the lawyer — "desiring to justify himself" — asks the follow-up that triggers the parable: "And who is my neighbor?" A priest and a Levite, both with plausible ritual-purity reasons to avoid a bloodied body, pass by; a Samaritan — a member of a group Jews of the day despised — stops, treats the man's wounds, and pays for his ongoing care.
Jesus answers "who is my neighbor?" by making the hated outsider the hero, and then reframes the question entirely: not who qualifies as your neighbor, but whether you acted like one.

⚖️ The Lawyer's Test

A question meant to trap or justify Jesus turns into a question that traps the lawyer instead — his follow-up assumes some people don't count as "neighbor."

🚶 Priest & Levite Pass By

Both had defensible religious reasons (corpse-impurity laws) to avoid the man — the parable doesn't explain their motives, leaving the omission itself as the indictment.

🩹 The Samaritan Stops

Crosses every social and religious boundary of the day to bind wounds, use his own oil and wine, and personally cover the cost of recovery at an inn.

❓ The Reversed Question

Jesus doesn't answer "who is my neighbor" — he asks "which of these three proved to be a neighbor?", turning a question about limits into a question about action.

🟡 Why traditions diverge here

The ethical core — radical, boundary-crossing neighbor-love, regardless of who's asking or who's in need — is shared across essentially every tradition. The divergence is about how far past that single point to read the story's details.

This is the single most famous case study in allegorical interpretation: Augustine read the wounded man as Adam/humanity, Jerusalem as the heavenly city, Jericho as mortality, the robbers as the devil, the priest and Levite as the Old Testament law and priesthood unable to save, the Samaritan as Christ, the inn as the Church, and the two coins as either the two great commandments or the sacraments. Echoes of this reading persist in some Catholic and Orthodox catechesis and liturgical use of the parable. Reformers and most Evangelicals, wary of allegory not clearly signaled by the text itself, read the parable as a single, direct moral example about neighbor-love and stop there — treating the fuller allegorical reading as edifying tradition rather than the parable's actual meaning.
