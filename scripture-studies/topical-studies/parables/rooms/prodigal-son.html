The Prodigal Son

Luke 15:11–32

🟢 High Consensus
Context. The third and climactic of three "lost" parables in Luke 15 — the lost sheep, the lost coin, the lost son — all given in response to Pharisees and scribes grumbling that Jesus "receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2). The parable is really about two sons and one father, and the audience is meant to feel the sting of the second half as much as the comfort of the first.
A father runs to meet the son who squandered everything — and then has to plead with the son who never left.

💸 The Request & Ruin

The younger son demands his inheritance early — effectively wishing his father dead — then loses it all in "riotous living" in a far country, ending up feeding pigs, the ultimate degradation for a Jewish audience.

🔄 Coming to Himself

Starving, he rehearses a confession and heads home expecting, at best, to be taken on as a hired servant — not restored as a son.

🏃 The Father's Welcome

While the son is still far off, the father sees him and runs — an undignified act for a patriarch — cutting off the rehearsed speech with a robe, a ring, sandals, and a feast.

😠 The Elder Son

Refuses to join the celebration, protests his own unrewarded years of obedience, and is met with the father's plea rather than a rebuke. The parable ends unresolved — we never learn if he goes in.

🟢 Why this one holds together across traditions

The central image — a father whose love runs out to meet repentance before a word of apology is even finished — is about as close to universal agreement as this app finds anywhere. Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Baptist, Mainline, and Evangelical readings all converge on grace freely extended to the undeserving as the parable's core.

The only real shading, not a genuine split: Catholic tradition has often tied this parable specifically to the sacrament of confession and reconciliation (the son's rehearsed confession mirroring sacramental contrition), while Protestant traditions read the same confession simply as the shape repentance takes, without a sacramental frame attached. The elder son's unresolved ending is also near-universally read as a pointed warning to self-righteous religious insiders — one of the more consistently agreed-upon "sting in the tail" readings in all of Jesus's parables.
