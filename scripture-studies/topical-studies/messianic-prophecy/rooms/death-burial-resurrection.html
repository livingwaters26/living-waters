Death, Burial &amp; Resurrection

Prophecies fulfilled at the cross, the tomb, and the empty tomb

The crucifixion narrative contains the single densest cluster of Old Testament fulfillment in the entire Bible — much of it from Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53 alone — followed by the resurrection and ascension every tradition treats as the hinge of the Christian faith.

📖 Passages in Full
✝️

1. Hands and feet pierced (Psalm 22:16 → Luke 23:33; John 20:25)

Prophecy
For dogs have compassed me: A company of evil-doers have inclosed me; They pierced my hands and my feet.
Fulfillment
And when they came unto the place which is called The skull, there they crucified him... Except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails... I will not believe.
Crucifixion as an execution method did not exist in David's era (it was developed by the Persians and perfected by the Romans centuries later) — making this psalm's specific imagery, written roughly 1,000 years before crucifixion existed, a frequently cited point in Christian apologetics.
😂

2. Mocked, wagging their heads (Psalm 22:7–8 → Matthew 27:39–43)

Prophecy
All they that see me laugh me to scorn: They shoot out the lip, they shake the head, saying, Commit thyself unto Jehovah; let him deliver him: Let him rescue him, seeing he delighteth in him.
Fulfillment
And they that passed by railed on him, wagging their heads, and saying... He trusteth on God; let him deliver him now, if he desireth him.
Matthew records the mockers' words closely enough to the psalm's own phrasing that most interpreters believe the crowd, familiar with Psalm 22, was likely mocking Jesus with conscious awareness of the psalm itself.
🎲

3. Garments divided by lot (Psalm 22:18 → John 19:23–24)

Prophecy
They part my garments among them, And upon my vesture do they cast lots.
Fulfillment
They said therefore one to another, Let us not rend it, but cast lots for it, whose it shall be: that the scripture might be fulfilled, which saith, They parted my garments among them, And upon my vesture did they cast lots.
John is the only Gospel writer who explicitly cites this psalm text by name at this exact moment — an intentional, direct citation rather than an implied allusion.
😢

4. "My God, why hast thou forsaken me" (Psalm 22:1 → Matthew 27:46)

Prophecy
My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my groaning?
Fulfillment
And about the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani? that is, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?
Psalm 22 turns from anguish (vv.1–21) to triumphant praise and worldwide worship (vv.22–31) — many interpreters across traditions believe Jesus, by quoting the opening line, was invoking the entire psalm's trajectory from abandonment to vindication, not despair alone.
🍷

5. Given vinegar and gall (Psalm 69:21 → Matthew 27:34, 48)

Prophecy
They gave me also gall for my food; And in my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink.
Fulfillment
They gave him wine to drink mingled with gall... and one of them straightway ran, and took a sponge, and filled it with vinegar, and put it on a reed, and gave him to drink.
Matthew records two separate offerings (27:34, an early gall-laced wine He refuses, and 27:48, vinegar near the end He appears to accept) — most harmonizations see these as two distinct moments each independently echoing this psalm.
🦴

6. Not a bone broken (Psalm 34:20; Exodus 12:46 → John 19:33, 36)

Prophecy
He keepeth all his bones: Not one of them is broken.
Fulfillment
But when they came to Jesus, and saw that he was dead already, they brake not his legs... For these things came to pass, that the scripture might be fulfilled, A bone of him shall not be broken.
John ties this to both Psalm 34:20 and the Passover lamb regulation in Exodus 12:46 — reinforcing the Gospel's broader identification of Jesus as the true Passover Lamb, crucified during Passover week itself.
🗡️

7. They shall look on him whom they pierced (Zechariah 12:10 → John 19:34, 37)

Prophecy
...and they shall look unto me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him, as one mourneth for his only son...
Fulfillment
Howbeit one of the soldiers with a spear pierced his side... For these things came to pass, that the scripture might be fulfilled... They shall look on him whom they pierced.
This same verse is applied a second time, to the Second Coming, in Revelation 1:7 — one prophecy explicitly cited for two separate eschatological moments, first-century crucifixion and future return.
⚰️

8. A grave with the rich, though innocent (Isaiah 53:9 → Matthew 27:57–60)

Prophecy
And they made his grave with the wicked, and with a rich man in his death; although he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth.
Fulfillment
And when even was come, there came a rich man from Arimathaea, named Joseph... and laid it in his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock.
This verse holds together two burial details that would otherwise seem contradictory — dying among the wicked (the two thieves) yet buried with the rich (Joseph of Arimathea) — matched precisely by Matthew's account.
🌅

9. Not abandoned to the grave (Psalm 16:10 → Acts 2:25–31)

Prophecy
For thou wilt not leave my soul to Sheol; Neither wilt thou suffer thy holy one to see corruption.
Fulfillment
He foreseeing this spake of the resurrection of the Christ, that neither was he left unto Hades, nor did his flesh see corruption.
Peter's argument is explicitly textual and logical, not just devotional: since David did decay in his tomb (Acts 2:29), David must have been speaking prophetically about someone else's resurrection — a key early apologetic argument for the empty tomb.
🕊️

10. Ascended on high (Psalm 68:18 → Ephesians 4:8; Acts 1:9–11)

Prophecy
Thou hast ascended on high, thou hast led away captives; Thou hast received gifts among men...
Fulfillment
Wherefore he saith, When he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, And gave gifts unto men.
Paul adapts the psalm's wording from "received gifts" to "gave gifts" — a change every tradition addresses, generally understood as Paul drawing out the psalm's implication (a victorious king redistributes plunder to his own people) rather than misquoting it.
