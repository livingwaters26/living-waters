The Olivet Discourse

Matthew 24 · Mark 13 · Luke 21

Jesus's disciples point out the Jerusalem temple's stones; he responds that not one will be left on another (Matt 24:2) — then, sitting on the Mount of Olives, delivers his longest sustained teaching on the future. The disciples' question in Matthew's version has two parts: "when shall these things be?" (the temple's destruction) and "what shall be the sign of thy coming, and of the end of the world?" — and one of the central interpretive puzzles of the whole discourse is figuring out which answer applies to which question, since Jesus doesn't clearly separate them the way a modern outline would.

📋 What the Discourse Covers

Warning signs

False messiahs, wars and rumors of wars, famines, earthquakes — "the beginning of sorrows" (Matt 24:4-8), explicitly not yet the end itself.

Persecution and the gospel's spread

Disciples handed over to tribulation and death, but the gospel preached to all nations first (Matt 24:9-14) — read by many as already substantially underway in the apostolic period, whatever its final completion looks like.

The abomination of desolation

A direct citation of Daniel 9:27/11:31/12:11, with a command to flee Judea when it appears (Matt 24:15-22) — the passage most directly tied to Jerusalem's actual fall in 70 AD, when Roman armies (the "abomination" in Luke's parallel, 21:20, explicitly identified as "Jerusalem compassed with armies") desecrated and destroyed the temple.

Cosmic signs and the Son of Man's coming

Sun darkened, stars falling, then the Son of Man coming on clouds with power and glory (Matt 24:29-31) — stock apocalyptic language (see Reading Apocalyptic Literature) applied here to what most traditions read as either Jerusalem's fall described in cosmic terms, or Christ's final return, or both layered together.

🧩 The "This Generation" Puzzle

"Verily I say unto you, This generation shall not pass, till all these things be fulfilled" (Matt 24:34) sits right after the cosmic-signs/Son-of-Man passage — creating real tension if that passage describes a still-future final return, since Jesus's own generation has obviously passed. Four main resolutions are in live use across the traditions this site compares:

Preterist reading

"All these things" — including the cosmic-language section — refers to Jerusalem's fall in 70 AD, well within that generation's lifetime. This is the most straightforward reading of "this generation" as Jesus's own contemporaries, and the strongest argument for at least a partial-preterist approach to this specific passage.

Futurist reading

"Generation" (Greek genea) is read as "race" or "people" — meaning the Jewish people, or humanity generally, will endure until these final events occur, rather than pinning it to a 30-40 year window. A minority linguistic argument, but consistently used in Dispensational and other futurist readings to keep the whole passage future.

Split-reference reading

The discourse itself shifts referents partway through — earlier material (through the abomination of desolation) genuinely is about 70 AD and fits "this generation" exactly; later material (the Son of Man's visible coming, the final judgment) shifts to describe Christ's still-future return, functioning as a "near/far" prophetic pattern comparable to how some Old Testament prophecies blend a near and a far fulfillment in one oracle.

Idealist/typological reading

Jerusalem's fall functions as a recurring type of divine judgment and vindication throughout history, of which the final judgment is the ultimate instance — "this generation" describes the certainty and nearness of judgment as a pattern, not a strict chronological deadline for one single event.

⚖️ No verdict is given here on which resolution is correct — this is a genuinely contested question among careful, orthodox interpreters across every tradition this site compares, not a case of one side simply not having read the text closely.
