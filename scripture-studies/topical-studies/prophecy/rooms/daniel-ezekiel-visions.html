Daniel & Ezekiel's Visions

Where biblical apocalyptic's vocabulary comes from

Both prophets wrote from exile — Ezekiel among the first deportees in Babylon, Daniel across the Babylonian and Persian courts. Exile is the seedbed of apocalyptic generally: when a nation's own political and religious institutions have collapsed, a genre emerges that looks past current events entirely to God's sovereign, decisive action outside the ordinary political order. Revelation, written to churches under Roman pressure, draws on both books constantly — more Old Testament allusions per chapter than almost any other New Testament book, and a large share of them come from here.

📜 Daniel's Core Visions

Daniel 2 — The Statue of Four Kingdoms

Nebuchadnezzar's dream: gold, silver, bronze, and iron-and-clay, struck down by a stone "cut without hands." Almost universally read as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome (with the divided feet variously read as Rome's later division or a still-future final form) — one of the few sequences with broad cross-tradition agreement on the historical referents themselves, even where the stone's timing (already established vs. still future) is read differently.

Daniel 7 — The Four Beasts

The same four kingdoms retold as a lion, bear, leopard, and a terrifying nameless fourth beast with iron teeth and ten horns — direct source imagery for Revelation 13's composite beast. The "Ancient of Days" and "one like a son of man" given an everlasting kingdom (7:13-14) is among the most direct Old Testament roots of Jesus's own favorite self-title.

Daniel 9 — The Seventy Weeks

A prophecy of "seventy weeks" (of years — 490 years) determined for Jerusalem, containing precise-sounding markers for "Messiah the Prince." Genuinely contested rather than settled: readings range from a straightforward run to Christ's first coming, to a final unfulfilled "seventieth week" still future (standard in Dispensational futurism), to various other chronological schemes. Flagged here as a real, live disagreement rather than glossed over.

Daniel 12 — Resurrection and the End

"Many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" — one of the clearest, earliest Old Testament statements of bodily resurrection, some to everlasting life and some to shame. Sets up the resurrection hope the New Testament assumes throughout.

👁️ Ezekiel's Core Visions

Ezekiel 1 — The Throne-Chariot Vision

Four living creatures (later echoed in Revelation 4's four living creatures), wheels within wheels, and a vision of God's glory that moves — the theological point being that God's presence isn't chained to the Jerusalem temple, even in exile. Foundational for the entire later Jewish mystical tradition of "chariot" (merkabah) contemplation.

Ezekiel 37 — The Valley of Dry Bones

Dry bones reassembled and given breath — explicitly interpreted within the passage itself (37:11-14) as Israel's national restoration from exile, not primarily individual resurrection, though the image has always carried resurrection overtones too.

Ezekiel 38-39 — Gog and Magog

A climactic invasion by a coalition led by "Gog of the land of Magog," defeated decisively by God — reused directly in Revelation 20:8 for the final rebellion after the thousand years. Whether Ezekiel's and Revelation's "Gog and Magog" refer to the same event or Revelation is reusing the name for a distinct later one is itself a live question across the premil/amil/postmil readings covered in Millennial Kingdom Prophecy.

Ezekiel 40-48 — The Visionary Temple

A detailed, measured temple with a river of healing flowing from it (47:1-12) — read across this site's traditions as a literal future millennial temple, a symbolic picture of restored worship, or a typological pointer to Christ and the church, without a single verdict imposed. The river imagery directly feeds Revelation 22's river of life.
