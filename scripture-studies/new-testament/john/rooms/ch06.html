John 6

The Bread of Life

A miraculous feeding of five thousand becomes, the next day, the occasion for Jesus's most demanding and most debated discourse — "I am the bread of life," and the startling claim that His followers must eat His flesh and drink His blood. No single passage does more to explain why Christians read the Lord's Supper so differently.

The Feeding of the Five Thousand

John 6:1-15
9There is a lad here, which hath five barley loaves, and two small fishes: but what are they among so many?... 11And Jesus took the loaves; and when he had given thanks, he distributed to the disciples... 13Therefore they gathered them together, and filled twelve baskets with the fragments... 14Then those men, when they had seen the miracle that Jesus did, said, This is of a truth that prophet that should come into the world.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six read this as a real, historical, miraculous multiplication of food — one of the few miracles recorded in all four Gospels — that directly sets up the "true bread" discourse following it.
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: Little direct doctrinal disagreement on the miracle itself; traditions vary somewhat in how strongly they connect its imagery typologically forward to the Lord's Supper Jesus institutes later.
Catholic
Catholic commentary reads the crowd's "prophet that should come" (echoing Deut 18:15-18) alongside the twelve baskets of fragments (often connected to the twelve tribes/apostles) as establishing Jesus as both the promised Prophet and the true, abundant provider for His people — themes carried directly into the Bread of Life discourse and ultimately the Eucharist.

Armstrong's material notes John's specific detail that this took place "nigh unto the passover" (v.4) as deliberately setting the whole chapter, including the hard sayings to come, against a Passover backdrop — the same festival during which Jesus will later institute the Lord's Supper.

The Bread of Life Discourse

John 6:22-59
35And Jesus said unto them, I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger... 51I am the living bread which came down from heaven: if any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever: and the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world... 54Whoso eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, hath eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day... 63It is the spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life.
🤝 Where the traditions agree: All six affirm Jesus is teaching that spiritual life comes only through vital, personal union with Him, and all take verse 63 seriously as Jesus's own necessary clarifying word on the hard sayings that precede it.
⚡ Where they diverge, and why: This is the sharpest sacramental fault line anywhere in the New Testament — whether "eating my flesh, drinking my blood" is primarily and directly about the Eucharist (a strongly sacramental reading) or describes saving faith in Christ's atoning death, illustrated by but conceptually prior to the Lord's Supper practice that would later dramatize it (the majority Protestant/evangelical reading) — directly paralleling the Church History room's transubstantiation debate and Matthew 26's Last Supper.
Catholic
The Catechism (CCC 1373-1381) reads this discourse as Christ's own direct teaching preparing for and undergirding the Real Presence in the Eucharist — the crude verb "gnaw" (trōgō) John switches to at v.54 is cited by Catholic commentators as evidence against a merely symbolic reading, since Jesus notably does not soften His language when many disciples are offended and leave (v.60-66), read by Catholic tradition as confirming He meant something startlingly, physically real rather than correcting a misunderstanding.
