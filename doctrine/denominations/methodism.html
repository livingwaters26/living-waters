Methodism

A holiness revival within the Church of England that became its own global denomination

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figures
John Wesley (chief architect) and Charles Wesley (hymn writer); George Whitefield as an early evangelistic partner
Formative Era
The 1729 Oxford "Holy Club" through Wesley's 1791 death and Methodism's formal separation from Anglicanism
Governance
Connectional — bishops and conferences in most branches, tightly organized circuits historically
Key Documents
Wesley's Sermons and Notes on the New Testament, the Articles of Religion (an adapted Thirty-Nine Articles)

🕰️ Historical Origin

John and Charles Wesley, along with George Whitefield, formed the "Holy Club" at Oxford in 1729 — a small group committed to disciplined spiritual practice that earned the mocking nickname "Methodists" for their methodical devotional habits. John Wesley remained an ordained Anglican priest his entire life and never intended to found a separate denomination; Methodism began as a revival and discipleship movement within the Church of England, organized into small accountability groups ("class meetings") and traveling preaching circuits.

Wesley's theology broke from the Reformed mainstream on the nature of grace: he held to Arminian synergism (a genuine human response to God's initiating, "prevenient" grace is possible and necessary) rather than Calvinist unconditional election, and taught that "entire sanctification" — a decisive, Spirit-wrought growth in holiness beyond initial conversion — was a real, attainable Christian experience. Methodism formally separated from the Church of England only after Wesley's death in 1791, becoming an independent denomination in both Britain and America.

✦ Core Distinctives

Prevenient Grace

God's grace goes before and enables a genuine human response to the gospel — a synergistic (Arminian) alternative to Calvinist irresistible grace, while still insisting grace initiates everything.

Entire Sanctification

Believers can experience a decisive further work of grace after conversion, growing in holiness and love toward Christian perfection in this life — Methodism's most distinctive doctrinal contribution.

The Wesleyan Quadrilateral

Theological reflection draws on Scripture (primary), tradition, reason, and Christian experience together — a framework named after Wesley though systematized more fully by later Methodist scholars.

Social Holiness

Wesley insisted personal holiness and social action belong together — driving Methodism's historic involvement in prison reform, abolition, and care for the poor.
