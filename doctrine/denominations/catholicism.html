Catholicism

The largest single Christian body, tracing an unbroken line of bishops back to the apostles

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figure
Peter (traditional first Bishop of Rome); no single founder beyond the apostolic church itself
Formative Era
Apostolic age onward, with identity sharpened by the 1054 Schism and 1517 Reformation
Governance
Papal — Pope, then bishops (in communion with Rome), then priests and deacons
Key Councils
Nicaea (325), Trent (1545-63), Vatican I (1869-70), Vatican II (1962-65)

🕰️ Historical Origin

Catholic self-understanding traces an unbroken chain of ordination and teaching authority (apostolic succession) from Peter, traditionally the first Bishop of Rome, through every subsequent pope. For roughly a thousand years the church was substantially united across East and West, until the 1054 Great Schism formally split Rome from Constantinople over the filioque clause, papal authority, and mounting cultural and political divergence.

The 1517 Reformation was the next major rupture — not a split from Orthodoxy but from within Western (Latin) Christianity itself. The Council of Trent (1545-1563) was Catholicism's decisive response, clarifying doctrine on justification, the sacraments, and the biblical canon (including affirming the deuterocanonical books) against Protestant positions. Vatican I (1869-70) defined papal infallibility under specific conditions; Vatican II (1962-65) modernized liturgy and the church's posture toward other Christians and the modern world without altering core doctrine.

✦ Core Distinctives

Petrine Succession

The Pope holds a unique teaching authority (the Magisterium) as the successor of Peter, exercised alongside — not instead of — Scripture and Sacred Tradition.

Seven Sacraments

Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Reconciliation, Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Matrimony — each a real means of grace, not merely a symbolic act.

Transubstantiation

The bread and wine become the actual body and blood of Christ at consecration, while retaining their physical appearance — formally defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and reaffirmed at Trent.

Justification

Grace-initiated, but genuinely cooperative — faith expressed through love and good works, not faith alone as a legal declaration only.
