Anabaptism

The Radical Reformation's insistence on believer's church and strict separation from worldly power

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figures
Conrad Grebel, Felix Manz, and George Blaurock (the first "Swiss Brethren" re-baptisms, Zurich, 1525)
Formative Era
1525 in Zurich, through the 1527 Schleitheim Confession
Governance
Congregational, strongly decentralized, with a historic emphasis on communal discipline
Key Documents
The Schleitheim Confession (1527)

🕰️ Historical Origin

Anabaptism ("re-baptizers" — originally a hostile label, since infant baptism was considered valid by both Catholics and other early Protestants) began in Zurich in 1525 when Conrad Grebel and companions, once students of Zwingli, concluded the Reformed reforms weren't going far enough. They baptized each other as adult believers, rejecting infant baptism outright and insisting the true church must be a voluntary community of the genuinely converted, entirely separate from state control — a position that put them at odds with essentially every other authority of the era, Catholic and Protestant alike.

The movement faced severe, sustained persecution from both Catholic and mainline Protestant authorities — Felix Manz himself was drowned by Zurich's city council in 1527, a grim irony given the group's baptismal practice. The Schleitheim Confession (1527), largely authored by Michael Sattler, became the movement's foundational statement: believer's baptism, church discipline (the "ban"), a memorial view of communion, strict separation from worldly society and government office, and nonresistance (pacifism) even in self-defense. Later Anabaptist branches include the Mennonites (after Menno Simons) and, later still, the Amish (after a further split led by Jakob Ammann in 1693).

✦ Core Distinctives

Believer's Church

The church must consist only of voluntary, adult believers who have chosen baptism and discipleship — never an inherited, whole-society church membership.

Church-State Separation

A strict, foundational separation between church and civil government — refusing state church support and, historically, refusing to hold civil office at all.

Nonresistance

A historic commitment to pacifism, refusing violence even in self-defense or civil military service — rooted in a literal reading of Jesus's teaching on non-retaliation.

Simplicity and Community Discipline

A strong emphasis on communal accountability (the ban, or shunning, for unrepentant sin) and, in later Mennonite/Amish branches, deliberate simplicity of lifestyle.
