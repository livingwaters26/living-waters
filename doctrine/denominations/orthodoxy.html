Eastern Orthodoxy

The Eastern half of the ancient undivided church, organized as communion of self-governing national churches

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figure
No single founder; traces continuous life from the apostolic church in the Greek East
Formative Era
The seven Ecumenical Councils, 325-787 AD, then formal separation from Rome in 1054
Governance
Conciliar — a communion of autocephalous (self-governing) national churches, no single earthly head
Key Councils
Nicaea I (325) through Nicaea II (787) — the seven Ecumenical Councils held before the East-West split

🕰️ Historical Origin

Orthodoxy understands itself not as a breakaway church but as the continuation of the ancient, undivided church in its Eastern, Greek-speaking form — centered historically on Constantinople rather than Rome. The seven Ecumenical Councils, from Nicaea (325) through Nicaea II (787), settled the core Trinitarian and Christological questions that all of historic Christianity still affirms today.

The 1054 Great Schism formally divided East from West over several compounding issues: the filioque clause added to the Nicene Creed in the West (whether the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, or from the Father "and the Son"), the scope of papal authority (Rome's claim to universal jurisdiction versus the East's conciliar model), and centuries of accumulated cultural and political estrangement between the Greek East and Latin West. Hesychasm — a tradition of contemplative prayer aimed at direct experiential union with God's uncreated energies — developed distinctively in the East and was formally defended by Gregory Palamas in the 14th century.

✦ Core Distinctives

Theosis

Salvation is understood as progressive union with God's energies (not His essence) — becoming by grace what Christ is by nature — rather than primarily a legal declaration of righteousness.

Holy Tradition

Scripture is read within the living Tradition of the church — the councils, liturgy, and the Fathers — rather than as a self-sufficient standalone authority.

The Seven Mysteries

Sacraments (called Mysteries) are affirmed with real spiritual efficacy, without committing to Western Aristotelian categories like transubstantiation to explain how.

Icons and Liturgy

Icons are venerated (not worshipped) as windows into the sacred, defended at the Second Council of Nicaea (787) after the earlier Iconoclast controversy.
