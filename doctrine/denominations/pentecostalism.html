Pentecostalism

The newest major branch of Christianity, centered on the ongoing experience of Spirit baptism

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figure
William J. Seymour, an African American Holiness preacher, leading the Azusa Street Revival
Formative Era
The Azusa Street Revival, Los Angeles, 1906-1915
Governance
Varies widely by denomination — congregational to episcopal, no single unifying structure
Key Event
Azusa Street itself functions as Pentecostalism's founding event rather than a single confessional document

🕰️ Historical Origin

Pentecostalism emerged from the Holiness movement, itself a 19th-century offshoot of Methodism emphasizing entire sanctification as a definite, datable experience. Charles Parham, a Holiness preacher in Topeka, Kansas, taught in 1901 that speaking in tongues (glossolalia) was the necessary initial evidence of Spirit baptism. His student William Seymour, an African American preacher, carried this teaching to Los Angeles, where the Azusa Street Revival broke out in 1906 — a sustained, racially integrated (highly unusual for the era) revival meeting marked by tongues, healing claims, and ecstatic worship that ran nearly continuously for years and drew visitors from around the world.

From Azusa Street, Pentecostalism spread rapidly into a wide range of denominations (Assemblies of God, Church of God in Christ, and many others) and later, from the 1960s onward, into a broader "charismatic" movement within existing mainline Protestant and even Catholic circles — Christians who embraced Pentecostal-style spiritual gifts while remaining within their original denominations. Pentecostalism and its charismatic offshoots are now, by most estimates, among the fastest-growing branches of global Christianity, especially across Africa, Latin America, and Asia.

✦ Core Distinctives

Baptism in the Holy Spirit

A distinct experience subsequent to conversion, evidenced (in classical Pentecostal teaching) by speaking in tongues — the movement's founding and defining doctrine.

Continuationism

Spiritual gifts described in the New Testament — tongues, prophecy, healing — are understood to continue actively today, against the "cessationist" view (more common in Reformed circles) that such gifts ended with the apostolic era.

Expressive, Spirit-Led Worship

Worship style historically emphasizes spontaneity, physical expression, and openness to real-time prophetic or charismatic manifestation, distinct from more liturgically fixed traditions.

Rapid Global Growth

Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity has grown explosively since 1906, particularly in the Global South, reshaping world Christianity's overall demographic center.
