Lutheranism

The first major Reformation tradition, built on justification by faith alone

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figure
Martin Luther, an Augustinian monk and university professor at Wittenberg
Formative Era
1517 Ninety-Five Theses through the 1530 Augsburg Confession
Governance
Varies by region/synod — episcopal in some national churches, synodical in others
Key Documents
The Augsburg Confession (1530), Luther's Small and Large Catechisms

🕰️ Historical Origin

Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses (1517), posted to protest the sale of indulgences, ignited the wider Reformation — though Luther's own core theological breakthrough was earlier and deeper: his reading of Romans 1:17 convinced him that righteousness before God comes by faith alone (sola fide), not through sacramental participation plus merit. Summoned to defend his views at the Diet of Worms (1521), Luther refused to recant unless convinced by Scripture or clear reason, famously (per later tradition) declaring "here I stand."

Excommunicated and placed under imperial ban, Luther was protected by sympathetic German princes and continued writing, translating the New Testament into German (1522) to put Scripture directly in ordinary believers' hands. The Augsburg Confession (1530), primarily authored by Luther's colleague Philip Melanchthon, became Lutheranism's foundational confessional document, presented to Emperor Charles V as a statement of what the reforming churches actually believed — distinguishing Lutheran doctrine from both Rome and the more radical reformers.

✦ Core Distinctives

Sola Fide

Justification is by faith alone, apart from works — the material principle of the Reformation and Lutheranism's defining doctrine.

Sola Scriptura

Scripture alone holds final doctrinal authority, over church tradition or papal decree — the formal principle underlying sola fide.

Consubstantiation

Christ is genuinely present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine at communion — a real presence, but distinct from Catholic transubstantiation's change of substance.

Law and Gospel

Scripture is read through the distinction between Law (which exposes sin and demands obedience) and Gospel (which offers free forgiveness) — a hermeneutical lens distinctly emphasized in Lutheran theology.
