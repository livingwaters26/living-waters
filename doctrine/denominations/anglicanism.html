Anglicanism

The Church of England and its worldwide Communion, deliberately holding a middle path between Catholic and Protestant

📋 Quick Facts

Key Figures
King Henry VIII (political break from Rome); Thomas Cranmer (theological architect)
Formative Era
1534 Act of Supremacy, through the Elizabethan Settlement (1559) and the 1662 Book of Common Prayer
Governance
Episcopal; the Archbishop of Canterbury holds a symbolic "first among equals" primacy, not papal authority
Key Documents
The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571), the Book of Common Prayer

🕰️ Historical Origin

Henry VIII's 1534 Act of Supremacy broke the English church from papal authority, precipitated by the Pope's refusal to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon — a political rupture initially, more than a theological one. Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, became the movement's real theological architect, drafting the first Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552, 1662) and the Forty-Two (later Thirty-Nine) Articles, pulling the church in a more distinctly Reformed direction than Henry himself had intended.

The Elizabethan Settlement (1559), under Elizabeth I, established the lasting compromise: a church Catholic in structure and worship (bishops, liturgy, vestments) but Protestant in core doctrine (justification by faith, Scripture's final authority, two dominant sacraments) — the deliberate "Via Media" (middle way) that still defines Anglican identity. The Book of Common Prayer's 1662 revision became the enduring standard for worship across the global Anglican Communion.

✦ Core Distinctives

Via Media

A deliberate middle path — retaining Catholic liturgical form and episcopal structure while affirming Reformation-era doctrine on Scripture and salvation.

Scripture, Tradition, Reason

Authority rests on Scripture as primary, read through the lenses of church tradition and human reason — a three-legged framework distinct from strict sola scriptura.

The Book of Common Prayer

A single authorized liturgy shapes both public worship and personal devotion, functioning as a unifying document in a tradition without a single doctrinal authority like the papacy.

Broad Internal Range

Anglicanism spans Anglo-Catholic (closer to Rome liturgically), Evangelical (closer to Reformed Protestantism), and Broad Church wings, all within one communion — a range wider than most other traditions on this list.
