Old Testament Canonization

How the Hebrew Scriptures took the shape different traditions now hold

📖 The Threefold Tanakh Structure

Torah — "Instruction"

Genesis through Deuteronomy — the five books of Moses, universally recognized as authoritative earliest, likely by the time of Ezra (5th century BC).

Nevi'im — "Prophets"

Former Prophets (Joshua-Kings, essentially historical narrative) and Latter Prophets (Isaiah through Malachi) — recognized as authoritative by roughly the 2nd century BC, referenced as a settled collection in the prologue to Sirach (c. 132 BC).

Ketuvim — "Writings"

Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, Chronicles — the last section to close, with its exact boundaries the latest to fully settle.

📚 The Key Manuscript Witnesses

The Masoretic Text (MT)

The standard Hebrew text tradition, vocalized and precisely preserved by Jewish scribes (the Masoretes) between roughly 600-1000 AD. The base text behind virtually every modern Old Testament translation, Protestant and Catholic alike.

The Dead Sea Scrolls

Discovered 1947-1956 at Qumran, dating roughly 250 BC-70 AD — copies of nearly every OT book roughly a thousand years older than the previously oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts. Their major significance: they confirmed the Masoretic tradition's remarkable accuracy over that thousand-year gap, while also showing some textual variation existed in this earlier period.

The Septuagint (LXX)

A Greek translation begun in Alexandria, Egypt, around the 3rd century BC for the Torah, with the rest following over the next century or two. This is the Old Testament most New Testament writers actually quote from, and it included additional books beyond the later Hebrew/Masoretic canon — the direct historical source of the Apocrypha/deuterocanonical question below.

The Samaritan Pentateuch

The Samaritan community's own Hebrew text of the five books of Moses (only), preserved independently of the Masoretic tradition since their split from mainstream Judaism — useful as a third independent witness alongside the MT and LXX for the Torah specifically.

🕰️ How the Boundaries Were Settled

Ezra and the "Great Assembly" are credited by later Jewish tradition with an early role in collecting and standardizing the sacred texts, though the process was gradual rather than a single decisive act. The Jewish historian Josephus (c. 95 AD, Against Apion) is the earliest source to describe a closed 22/24-book count matching the later Hebrew canon almost exactly, suggesting the boundaries were substantially settled among mainstream Judaism well before then.

A rabbinic council traditionally associated with Jamnia (Yavneh), c. 90 AD, is often popularly cited as "closing" the Hebrew canon — but this is now considered an overstatement by most scholars; Jamnia-era rabbis discussed whether certain books (notably Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs) "defile the hands" (a technical phrase for canonical status), which shows debate continuing at the edges, not a single formal closing council in the way later Christian councils functioned.

⚖️ Protocanonical vs. Deuterocanonical

Because the Septuagint (Greek) circulated with additional books the later Hebrew/Masoretic tradition didn't include, and the early church largely used the Septuagint, these extra books stayed in Christian Old Testaments for over a millennium — including the original 1611 King James Version.

The Council of Trent (1546), responding directly to the Reformation, formally declared these books canonical for Catholics — "deuterocanonical" (second canon, meaning later-recognized-but-still-canonical, not lesser). Protestant Bible societies began dropping them from standard printings in the 1820s, following the Reformers' preference for the Hebrew canon's boundaries. Eastern Orthodoxy holds an even broader set than the Catholic list, varying somewhat by jurisdiction.
